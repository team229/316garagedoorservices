Garage door noises, and what each one is telling you.

Grinding points at rollers or bearings. A squeal is a dry hinge or roller stem. One loud bang is a spring breaking. A rhythmic clunk is a flat-spotted roller or a bent track. Rattling is loose hardware. Humming with no movement is the opener. Below: how to tell which one you have, and whether to grease it, book it, or stop using the door.

Most of what a garage door tells you is mechanical. The noise points at a part, the part tells you how urgent it is, and where in the cycle it happens narrows it further — a sound at the start of travel is a different fault from the same sound once per revolution.

Is the noise new, or has it always been there?

A door that has always been loud is a different problem from a door that got loud last week. Sudden noise means a component changed: a roller seized, a hinge dried out, a bracket loosened, or a spring let go. Steady noise that has been there for years is usually worn rollers and a dry hinge line.

Sudden is the one to act on. If the sound started this week and the door is also travelling unevenly, stop cycling it until you have watched the tracks.

Why is my garage door grinding?

A continuous metallic grind, worse at the start of travel, is nearly always rollers. Steel rollers with dry or seized bearings stop turning and start dragging along the track. The drag also loads the opener, so a grind left alone gets expensive in two places at once.

Less commonly it is inside the opener: a grind that comes from the motor head rather than the tracks, or one that runs on for a second after the door stops.

What to do: run the door and watch the rollers in the track. A roller that has stopped turning, or one with a visible flat spot, is the source. Lubricating a seized roller buys weeks, not years — the bearing is already gone.

Rollers are cheap to replace and are usually done as a set. The ones we pull out most often are original steel rollers on doors that have never been lubricated. If that is what you have, nylon replacements are the usual upgrade because they run quieter.

Why is my garage door squeaking?

A high-pitched squeal is friction, and the friction is usually a dry hinge, a dry roller stem or a torsion bar bearing. It tends to show up first in cold weather, when old lubricant thickens and stops doing its job.

What to do: silicone spray or white lithium grease on the hinges, roller stems and torsion bar bearings. Not on the tracks — they should stay clean and dry, because grease collects grit and grinds it into the rollers. And not WD-40 as a lubricant. It's a solvent, and it will wash out whatever grease was left.

If the squeal is back within a week, a bearing is failing and the part needs replacing rather than another coat.

What does one loud bang mean?

If you heard a single sharp crack and the door then felt heavy, stopped opening or would not stay up, a spring has failed. That's the one noise that means stop now. Running the opener on a broken spring can bend tracks, snap a cable and burn out the motor.

What to do: stop using the door. Do not try to lift it, and do not touch the springs or the cables. This is the one repair on this list that is never a homeowner job. See the warning signs of a broken garage door spring for what happens next.

Why does it clunk once per revolution?

A noise that repeats at the same point in every cycle is one damaged component passing a fixed point: a flat-spotted roller, a bent track section, a loose hinge or a panel that has shifted. It is the most useful noise on this list because it is predictable.

What to do: watch from a safe distance and note how far the door has travelled when it happens. A bent track can sometimes be straightened once; a flat-spotted roller gets replaced.

Why is my garage door rattling?

Rattling is loose hardware: hinge bolts, track brackets, the opener's hanging straps, or a torsion bar that has lost a set screw. It is the cheapest noise to fix and the one people live with longest.

What to do: with the door closed and the opener unplugged, check that track brackets and hinge bolts are snug. Snug, not gorilla-tight — over-tightening strips the holes in the panel and leaves you with a worse problem.

Why does the opener hum without moving the door?

The motor runs and nothing moves. Either the manual release is still engaged and the trolley is disconnected from the door, or the opener has a stripped gear or coupler. If the trolley travels along the rail but the door stays put, it is the release. If nothing moves at all, it is mechanical.

What to do: check the red release handle and re-engage the trolley. If it is engaged and the motor still runs without moving anything, the opener needs internal repair or replacement. Our control troubleshooting guide covers the rest of the opener symptoms.

Why is my garage door scraping?

Scraping is a door out of alignment: a roller sitting outside the track, a bent track, or a panel rubbing the jamb. It is the noise that turns into a stuck door if you ignore it.

What to do: stop and look before you run it again. If the door is crooked, that is an off-track problem, and forcing it turns a small adjustment into a track and panel replacement.

Is the noise coming from the door or the opener?

Some sounds are not the door at all: a chain drive with a dry or over-tight chain, a belt slapping a loose rail, or a motor with a failing bearing. Two checks settle it. If the noise carries on for a second after the door stops, or it still happens with the door disconnected, it is the opener.

The distinction matters because people replace openers to fix door problems. A worn roller on a dry hinge will make a quiet belt drive sound like something is coming apart — our opener buying guide covers that trap before you spend anything.

Why is my garage door only noisy in winter?

Cold thickens old lubricant, stiffens rollers and seals, and shrinks the tracks slightly, so a door that was marginal in July starts complaining in November. In the Lower Mainland that cold snap lands in late October or November, and it is when we get the noise calls. Cleaning and re-lubricating before it arrives removes most of the problem.

If the door is quiet when it first moves and then sticks to the floor, or the opener strains and gives up, that is ice rather than noise. A frozen door has its own fix, and forcing it is how bottom sections and cables get damaged.

What should a quiet garage door sound like?

A maintained sectional door makes a soft, even rumble: rollers rolling, the belt or chain moving, the door settling onto the floor. No scrape, no grind, no squeal, no bang. If yours has never been quiet, worn rollers and a dry hinge line usually explain all of it, and both are cheap parts. Most noisy garage doors are a maintenance problem rather than a worn-out door.

If you want the whole routine in one place, our maintenance checklist covers lubrication, balance and the monthly safety test. If the noise comes with a door that will not close, read the guide to a door that will not close instead — that cause is usually a sensor or a limit, not a bearing.

When should you stop using the door?

  • A bang, followed by a door that is heavy or will not stay up.
  • Grinding that continues after lubrication.
  • Scraping, or a door that is visibly crooked.
  • The opener hums with no movement after the release is re-engaged.
  • Any noise that started suddenly and comes with uneven travel.

Springs and cables are the two things you never touch. Watching rollers, tightening a bracket and spraying a hinge are homeowner jobs. Anything involving the torsion bar, the drums or the cables is not, whatever a video says — those parts hold enough force to injure or kill.

One more step after any repair: re-test the safety reverse. Lay a length of 2x4 flat on the floor and close the door — it has to reverse on contact, not press down on the wood — then pass your leg through the photo-eye beam while it is closing. Both behaviours are required by 16 CFR Part 1211, and both are worth rechecking on a door that has been running rough.

Common Questions

Questions about this guide.

Still stuck? Call 604-388-9868 and describe what is happening. We will point you the right way.

Why is my garage door suddenly so loud?

Sudden noise usually means a component has changed: a roller has seized, a hinge has dried out, a track bracket has loosened or a spring has failed. Steady noise that has always been there is usually worn rollers.

What does a grinding garage door mean?

Grinding is nearly always rollers with dry or seized bearings dragging on the track. If the sound comes from the motor head instead, it may be the opener's gear or rail.

Is it safe to use a noisy garage door?

A squeak or rattle can wait a few days. Grinding, scraping, or a bang followed by a heavy door should stop immediately, because continuing to cycle the door damages parts that were still fine.

Can I just lubricate a noisy garage door?

Lubrication fixes squeals and some rattles. It does not fix a seized roller, a bent track or a broken spring, and greasing the tracks makes grinding worse by trapping grit.

What causes a loud bang from the garage?

A torsion or extension spring breaking. Stop using the door, keep clear of it, and call a technician — springs are under enough tension to injure or kill.

Why does my door make noise only in winter?

Cold thickens old lubricant, stiffens rollers and seals, and shrinks the tracks slightly. Cleaning and lubricating before the first freeze usually removes most of it.

How do I make my garage door quieter?

Fix the cause before adding lubricant. Replace rollers that have seized or flat-spotted, tighten loose hinge and bracket bolts, then apply silicone spray or white lithium grease to the hinges, roller stems and torsion bearings. Keep the tracks clean and dry. If the door is still loud, nylon rollers run quieter than steel.
